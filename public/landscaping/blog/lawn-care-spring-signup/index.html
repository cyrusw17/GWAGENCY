Lawn care spring signup page: fill your route before March

Updated October 5, 2026

A lawn care spring signup page is one page on your website where a homeowner picks a plan, sees the price and the week you start, and sends you their address. Put it up in January, send people to it, and a good share of your route can be booked before the grass needs cutting. You also get to plan the route at the kitchen table in February, which beats planning it from the truck in April.

Why sign people up in winter

The main reason is route density. When signups come in over the winter, you can sort them by street and see where you have six lawns in a row and where you'd have one lonely yard fifteen minutes out. You can say yes to the first kind and politely pass on the second before you've promised anything.

It also helps you plan people and equipment. If you know in February that you're up thirty lawns on last year, there's still time to hire a helper or find another mower.

And every homeowner who signs up online in January is one less voicemail to return from somebody's back yard in March. People who weren't happy with last year's company have often decided by winter to switch, and an easy way to sign up gives them somewhere to go.

What goes on the page: plans, prices, start week, what's included

Keep the page short enough to read on a phone. Start with your plans, two or three at most. Here's an example layout; put your own numbers in:

  • Weekly mowing, April to October: from $[your price] a visit for a typical quarter-acre lot.
  • Every other week: from $[your price] a visit. If you only allow biweekly once growth slows down in summer, say so here.
  • Season plan: $[your price] a month, April through October, same weekly service, one even payment.

If you sell a monthly plan, show the math in one line so it doesn't look like a trick. As an example: "We plan on 28 weekly cuts. 28 visits at $[visit price], split over 7 months."

Under the prices, add one line on bigger lots: you measure and text a written price before the first cut. That way a homeowner with a corner lot and a hill doesn't think the "from" price is bait.

Next, the start week. "We start mowing the week of [date], weather depending" answers the question you'll hear more than any other in March.

Then what each visit includes: mow, edge along the drive and walks, string trim around beds and fences, blow clippings off the hard surfaces. Say whether you mulch or bag. If you offer add-ons like spring cleanup, aeration or fertilizing, list them with a "from" price and a note that they're optional.

Your rain and skip-a-week policy belongs here in a sentence or two. There's more on wording it in the winter checklist post.

The form: four fields

Ask for four things:

  1. Name.
  2. Service address. Label it that way, since some people own a rental or are signing up a parent's house.
  3. Phone or email, whichever they check. Let them fill in either one.
  4. Plan, as a dropdown with the plans from the page.

That's enough to measure the lot, price it and call them back. Gate codes and the dog you'll learn when you come out to measure. If you want one more box, make it an optional "anything we should know?" note. Every required field you add is one more reason for someone on their phone to give up halfway.

After they hit send, the page should tell them what happens next in plain words: "Got it. We'll text you within a day to set a time to measure. Nothing is charged until you've seen your price." Then do what it says. A signup that sits unanswered for a week in February goes cold.

Once a week, sort the new signups by street in a spreadsheet or your scheduling app, and you'll see the route take shape.

Early-bird offers that don't cheapen you

You don't need a big discount to get people to sign up early. Ten percent off in January mostly teaches customers to wait for the next sale, and it costs you on every cut all season.

Smaller offers work:

Lock last year's price for returning customers who sign up by a set date. If your prices are going up this year, this is a real reason to act now, and it doesn't touch your new rate.

Put early signups first in line for spring cleanups. Everyone wants their beds cleared and leaves gone in the first warm weeks, and you can't do every yard at once.

Hold their route day. A customer who's had Tuesdays for three years usually wants to keep Tuesdays.

Whatever you offer, give it a real deadline, like "sign up by [date]", and stick to it.

Getting last year's customers to it

Returning customers are your easiest signups, and most just need a reminder and a link. Text them in early January:

Hi [name], [you] from [company] here. We're booking the [year] season now. Your price stays at $[last year's price] a visit if you sign up by [date], and you keep your [day]: [link]

Two weeks later, send one follow-up to anyone who hasn't signed up. Make it easy to say no to:

Hi [name], just checking you saw this. Happy to keep your spot if you want it: [link]. If you're going a different way this year, no hard feelings.

For customers without a mobile number, mail a postcard. One side gets your name, a photo of one of your lawns and "Spring signups are open." The other side:

Your spot for [year] is open. Weekly mowing starts the week of [date]. Sign up by [date] and keep last year's price. Go to [your site]/signup or call or text [number].

Keep that web address short so people can type it from the card. Postcards work for new customers too: send the same card to houses on streets you already mow, since every new lawn there costs you almost no extra drive time.

Deposits: pros and cons

A deposit makes a signup more serious. Someone who has paid $[your deposit] is less likely to ghost you when you come to measure, and the money arrives in winter, when lawns aren't paying anything.

The downside is that fewer people finish the form, because a card number is a bigger ask than a name and address from someone who hasn't met you. You'll also need card payments set up on the site, you pay processing fees, and you need a clear refund rule for when you measure the lot and the price doesn't work for them, or you can't fit their street on the route.

A middle path many owners can live with: no deposit to sign up, then a small deposit credited to the first visit once they've agreed to the written price.

Whichever you pick, put the rule on the page in one line, such as "No payment until you approve your price."

FAQ: lawn care spring signup

When should the signup page go up?

January is a good target. Holiday bills are out of the way and you still have February to measure lots and set up the route. If your regulars usually call in December, put it up then.

Should I put prices on the signup page if every yard is different?

Yes, as "from" prices for a typical lot, with one line saying you measure and confirm the price before the first cut.

What if more people sign up than I can handle?

Take the streets that fit your route, start a waitlist for the rest, and tell people honestly where they stand. A long waitlist is also a sign your prices can go up next year. For what else homeowners look for before they sign up, see what to put on a lawn care website.
