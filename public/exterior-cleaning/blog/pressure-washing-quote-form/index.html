Pressure washing quote form: price jobs without driving out

Updated October 5, 2026

A pressure washing quote form that asks for the right things in the right order lets you price most house washes, driveways and roofs from your phone. You need two questions up front, three photos, a number you can text, and very little else. Below are the eight fields in order, the lines to put around the form so people finish it, and a reply you can send within the hour.

Think back over last week. How many times did you drive across town to look at a driveway, pace it off, give a number in the yard and never hear back? Each of those trips cost you fuel and a slot you could have filled with paid work. Some jobs still need eyes on them: a three-story with a steep roof, a commercial lot, a house where the owner swears the orange stains are "just dirt." Most residential work doesn't. A house wash gets priced on size, stories and how bad the siding is, and a homeowner holding a phone can show you all of that.

Most contact forms ask for a name, an email and a message, so you get "how much to clean my house?" and nothing else. Then you either drive out or start a text thread that dies after two messages.

Step 1: the two questions at the top of your pressure washing quote form

Start with what needs washing and where it is. Both are easy to answer, and both tell you right away whether the lead is worth chasing.

Make the first one a set of tap boxes: house, roof, driveway and walks, deck or fence, gutters, windows. List only what you actually sell. Boxes are quicker than typing on a phone, and now and then someone who came for the driveway ticks gutters too, which turns a small ticket into a better one without a second call.

The second is ZIP code. It tells you whether the job sits inside your drive limit, and it feels less personal than a street address on the first screen. You can get the full address once they've said yes to a price.

Right after those two, ask how many stories: one, two, or three and up. Story count changes your setup and how far you're reaching with the wand or the downstream, and on a lot of jobs it changes the price. Square footage helps on a house wash, but plenty of owners don't know it offhand (some will look it up on the county property record), so make it optional.

Step 2: the photos to ask for, and how to ask on a phone

Ask for three specific shots by name. A bare "upload photos" button gets you one blurry picture of a flower bed.

  1. The front of the house from the street, so you can see the whole face, the stories and the roofline.
  2. The driveway from the street end, so you can judge the length and see where the staining is worst.
  3. The worst spot up close: the green north wall, the black streaks on the roof, the oil stain, the rust from the sprinkler heads.

Put those three as short labels beside the upload button. Most people fill these forms out on a phone, so the button should open the camera or photo library and accept two or three pictures at once. If your form can't take uploads, write "Text your photos to [your number] after you hit send" and make the number a tap-to-text link.

The worst-spot photo usually tells you the most. You can see whether it's mildew that comes off with a normal soft wash, or rust and oil that need their own chemical and their own line on the quote.

The fields to leave out (and why long forms lose people)

Each extra field gives the homeowner another place to stop. They're often on the couch at night with two or three companies open, and if your form asks twelve questions while the next one asks four, the short one gets finished.

Leave these off the first form:

  • Full street address. ZIP is enough until you're scheduling.
  • Email and phone both required. Ask for a mobile number and say you'll text, with email as the fallback for people who won't give a number.
  • "How did you hear about us?" Ask on the job if you want to know.
  • Budget. Homeowners don't know what a house wash costs, and whatever they guess will be too low or will make them nervous.
  • Preferred dates and time windows. You'll settle the day by text once they've agreed to a price.
  • A required message box. Keep it optional. Some people will use it to tell you about the HOA letter, the gate code or the dog, which is worth reading.

That leaves eight fields: what needs washing, ZIP, stories, square footage (optional), photos, name, mobile number, and the optional notes box. On a phone, split them over two or three short screens. Services and ZIP go first, stories and photos next, contact details last, so the easy taps come before anything that feels like work.

What the page around the form should say

People finish the form when the words around it answer the questions they have right before they hit send. Put these lines above or beside it:

  • How fast you reply. Write "we text back the same day" only if you do, because a missed promise costs you more than saying nothing.
  • No obligation. One plain line, like "Free quote. Nobody shows up unless you book."
  • How your pricing works. For example: "House washes are priced by size and stories. Send photos and we'll text you a price." If you post starting prices on the site, put them here as well.
  • Insured, if you are. Offer to send the certificate, since HOAs and property managers often ask for one. If you don't carry insurance, leave the line off.
  • What happens after they send it. "You'll get a text from [your name] with a price. Reply to pick a day." People send more readily when they know what comes next.
  • What you do with their number. "We only use this to reply about your quote" handles the spam worry.

Keep Call and Text buttons close to the form for people who would rather send a photo from their camera roll than type.

Replying to a request: a text template to send within the hour

The form only pays off if you answer fast. Someone who sent three requests on a Tuesday night is likely to book with whoever comes back first with a real number. Save something like this in your phone and edit it per job. The bracketed prices are placeholders for your own.

Hi [first name], this is [your name] with [company]. Thanks for the photos. A soft wash of your [one/two]-story house in [town], siding, trim and soffits, is $[your price]. The driveway and front walk add $[your price]. That price holds on wash day unless we find something the photos didn't show, and we'd tell you before starting. I have [day] or [day] open next week. Does either work?

Naming the house and the town shows you looked at what they sent, and the price is a real number they can say yes to. Ending on two days to pick from is easier to reply to than "let me know."

When the photos aren't enough, ask for exactly what's missing: "Can you send one of the back of the house? I want to see the north side before I give you a number." That sounds careful, and you still haven't put a mile on the truck.

FAQ: pressure washing quote forms

Can I give an exact price online?

For a lot of jobs, yes, as long as you say what the price is based on. A two-car driveway or a one-story house wash from clear photos is straightforward. For roofs, three-story houses and stains you can't identify from a picture, give a "from" price or a range and confirm it before you start.

What if the photos are bad?

Ask for the one shot you need, by name. "Can you stand at the end of the driveway and take one of the whole house?" works better than "can you send more photos?" If they can't, offer a quick look the next time you're working nearby, so it isn't a special trip.
