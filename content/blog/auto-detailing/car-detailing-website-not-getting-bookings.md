# Why people look at your detailing site and don't book

When a car detailing website is not getting bookings but people are clearly visiting it, the site is losing them after they arrive. Your Google profile shows the website taps, the phone stays quiet, and somewhere before the booking button the customer gave up. You can fix that on the page without spending a dollar more on ads.

Below are the eight usual causes, each with a 30-second test you can run on your own phone right now, ideally in a parking lot on one bar of signal.

## They can't tell if you come to them

The first question anyone asks a mobile detailer is "do you come to me?" If the answer lives in the footer or on a map image, most people won't dig for it. They assume you don't and go back to the search results, where someone else has their town in the headline.

Shops have their own version of this: an address with nothing about where to park or leave the keys, or no address on the page at all.

The test: open your home page and don't scroll. Can you see the word "mobile" (or your street, if you have a bay) and at least one town name? Scroll once. Is there a plain list of the towns you cover? If you had to open a menu to find either, so did the customer.

## No price, so they assume it's expensive

Someone on the fence about a detail has a number in their head. If your site says "call for a quote," they have to guess yours, and people guess high. Texting a stranger to ask a price feels like the start of a negotiation, so plenty of them just don't.

You don't need a full price sheet. A "from" price for each main job, by vehicle size, gives them enough to decide whether to reach out. You keep room to adjust for the car that's been the dog's bed for three years, and you can say so right under the prices. There are three ways to lay this out in [how to show detailing prices on your website](/blog/how-to-price-car-detailing-on-website/).

The test: within one scroll of the top, can you find a dollar figure for a full detail on an SUV?

## The button is a form with nine fields

Count the boxes on your booking or quote form. Name, email, phone, address, year, make, model, color, preferred date, how did you hear about us. Each one makes sense on its own. Together they're a chore on a phone keyboard with a kid kicking the back of the seat.

To give a price and a time slot you mostly need a name, a phone number, the vehicle, the package and a ZIP code. Year and color can wait for the confirmation text. Ask for phone or email, whichever one you reply on.

If your booking tool makes people create an account before they see open times, check whether you can switch that off.

Then check the other end. When a form comes in, does your phone buzz within a minute, or does it sit in an inbox you open at night? By then a lot of those customers have booked whoever answered first.

The test: book yourself a job on your own site, one-handed, and time it. Over a minute, or one moment where you sighed, means a customer felt it too.

## It's slow on a phone

A page that loads fine on your home Wi-Fi can crawl on one bar of signal. The usual weight is photos uploaded straight off the camera, an autoplaying video at the top, and builder add-ons like chat bubbles and popups.

Google has a free tool for this called PageSpeed Insights:

1. On a computer, go to [pagespeed.web.dev](https://pagespeed.web.dev/).
2. Paste your home page address and press Analyze.
3. Make sure you're looking at the Mobile results.
4. Read the suggestions under the score. If photos are the problem, it usually lists the exact files that are too big.

There's a quicker test you can do from the driveway. Turn Wi-Fi off, open your site and count. If you're past two before your headline and a photo show up, it's worth fixing. Usually the fix is resizing photos to the size they show at and removing add-ons you forgot you installed.

## The photos are stock or too small

Someone comparing two detailers is judging the work. A stock photo of a black sports car under studio lights could be anyone's, and customers can tell it isn't yours. Tiny thumbnails that open into a slideshow aren't much better, because nobody taps through forty of them on a phone.

A handful of your own before-and-after pairs do more. Shoot each pair from the same angle in the same light, and show them big enough to see the stain gone or the swirls polished out. Put each pair next to the service it proves.

The test: without zooming in, can you tell what changed in every pair? And is every photo on the site a car you worked on?

## The reviews are hidden on another site

Plenty of detailers have good Google reviews and a website that says "Check out our reviews on Google." That sends the customer off your site at the moment they're deciding, and some of them tap into a competitor's listing on the way back.

Copy a few real reviews onto your page word for word, with first names and dates, and link to your Google profile for the rest. Never write reviews yourself or borrow them from another business.

The test: from your home page, can you read an actual review with a name on it without leaving your site?

## Old info (prices, hours, a closed location)

A price from two years ago. Hours that say you work Sundays when you stopped in the spring. The address of a bay you moved out of, or a holiday special that ended in January. Any one of these makes a customer wonder what else is wrong.

The test: read your site like a stranger would and check every price, hour, phone number and address against today. Then open your Google Business Profile and make sure its hours and phone number match the site. Put a reminder in your phone to do this at the start of each season.

## Nothing tells them what happens after they book

A first-time customer has a few worries a regular doesn't. Will someone confirm? Do I need to be home? Where do I leave the keys? When do I pay? If the site doesn't say, some people put off booking until they can call you, and then they forget.

A short "what happens next" note right under the booking button covers most of it. An example, to swap for your own policies: "You'll get a text within the hour to confirm your time. We bring our own water and power. You don't need to be home if you leave the keys where we agree. You pay after you've looked the car over."

The test: ask someone who has never booked you to read your page and tell you what happens after they press the button. Anything they have to guess at goes in the note.

## Car detailing website not getting bookings: what to fix first

Do the quick ones first. Most of them take less time than a maintenance wash.

- About ten minutes: correct old prices, hours and phone numbers, add the towns you cover near the top, and write the "what happens next" note.
- About an hour: cut the form down, copy a few reviews onto the page and add "from" prices.
- An afternoon: shoot before-and-after pairs to replace stock photos, and shrink the photos that slow the page down.

Then run the tests again from the parking lot. For everything a detailing page needs from top to bottom, in the order customers check it, see [what to put on a car detailing website](/blog/what-to-put-on-a-car-detailing-website/).
