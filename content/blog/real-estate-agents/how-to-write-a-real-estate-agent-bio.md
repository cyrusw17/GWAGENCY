# How to write a real estate agent bio people actually read

If you're working out how to write a real estate agent bio, write it for the person who already has your name. A friend told them to call you, and before they do, they open your About page. A bio that reads like a résumé ("top producer, 15 years of experience, committed to excellence") gives them nothing to go on. The structure below has four parts in a set order: you and the place, who you help, how you work, and then your credentials. Write it in first person, keep it short and save the numbers for the end.

## Why résumé bios don't work

NAR's [2025 Home Buyers and Sellers Generational Trends report](https://cms.nar.realtor/sites/default/files/2025-03/2025-home-buyers-and-sellers-generational-trends-report-04-01-2025.pdf) calls referrals "the primary method most buyers use to find their real estate agent," and 66% of recent sellers used a referral or an agent they'd worked with before. So the person reading your bio has usually heard about you from somebody. They aren't comparing sales totals across five agents. They want to know whether the person on the page matches what their friend described: easy to talk to, patient with a nervous first-time buyer, quick to call back.

A résumé bio can't tell them that. "Licensed since 2012, Multi-Million Dollar Club, member of the local board" could sit under any headshot in your office, so the reader skims it and either goes looking for your number or asks the friend for a second name. The whole page a referred client checks is covered in [what to put on a realtor website](/blog/what-to-put-on-a-realtor-website/). The bio is the part most agents rewrite three times and still don't like, so it gets its own walkthrough here.

## Part 1: you and the place

Open with two or three sentences on where you're from and how you ended up selling homes here. Give the reader one detail they could repeat to the friend who sent them, like "she used to teach middle school" or "he grew up two streets over from the hardware store." That's the line they'll remember after they close the tab.

Name the place the way locals do. If people say "the east side" or use a town's nickname, so should you.

Keep this part about you and your work in the area. "I've lived in this county for 20 years and sold homes in every corner of it" is about you. "It's a great place to raise kids" is a claim about who an area suits, and fair housing guidance warns agents away from that kind of line, in a bio or anywhere else. The same goes for schools, safety and places of worship.

## Part 2: who you help

"I help buyers and sellers" is true of every agent, so it tells the reader nothing. Name the situations you handle well: first-time buyers who want every step explained, people selling a house they inherited, owners downsizing after 30 years in one place, someone moving for a job who has to buy from across the country. Pick the two or three you're best at and say one thing you do for each.

Here's an example for a fictional agent: "A lot of my sellers are handling a parent's house. I'll work around your attorney's probate timeline, and I know the estate sale and cleanout crews people here actually use."

A reader in that spot sees their own situation and a reason to call. A reader who isn't in that spot skips to the next line, and that's fine.

## Part 3: how you work

Referred clients often ask their friend what you're like to work with, and most bios skip it. Say how you like to be reached, how fast you usually answer and what working with you looks like week to week. If you text back within the hour but go quiet during showings, say so. If you send buyers a short note after every showing, mention it.

Specific details do more here than warm ones. Nobody believes "I'm always available for my clients," because nobody is. "I answer texts until 9 p.m., Saturdays included" tells a buyer exactly what to expect.

Pace belongs here too, if it's part of how you work. Some agents run a quick, decisive search, and some are known for never pushing. If past clients keep telling you that you never rushed them, put that in your own words.

## Part 4: credentials, said plainly

Now the résumé part, and keep it short: license type and how long you've held it, your brokerage, any designations and the languages you work in. Spell out each designation the first time, since most sellers won't know what the letters after your name stand for.

Numbers belong here only if your MLS records back them up and you name the time period. A line like "I've helped [your number] households buy or sell since [year]" can be checked. "Top producer" with nothing behind it can't. Awards work the same way: name the award, who gave it and the year, or leave it off.

Your brokerage's advertising rules may require your brokerage name and license number near your name, so check those before you publish.

## Three short example bios (fictional agents)

These are written for made-up agents to show the structure in practice. Use them for the shape and fill in your own story.

### A newer agent

"I'm Jess Arden. I spent nine years running the front desk of a dental office, so I'm good at calming people down and keeping paperwork straight. I got my license in 2024 and work with [brokerage]. Most of my clients are first-time buyers, and I go through every form with you before you sign it. Text me anytime before 9 p.m. and I'll answer that day. I've closed four sales so far, two of them alongside my team lead, and I'll tell you which two."

### A long-time local agent

"I'm Ray Okafor, and I've sold homes on the north side since 2009. Before that I framed houses for a builder, so I look at the roof and the crawl space before the kitchen. A lot of my clients now are selling a house they've owned for decades, or handling a parent's estate. I'll help you decide what to fix and what to leave alone, and I'll give you the names of the contractors I'd call myself. Licensed broker with [brokerage]. I work in English and Igbo."

### A two-person team

"We're Dana and Luis Ferreira, and we've worked together at [brokerage] since 2016. Dana handles listings and pricing, and Luis handles buyers and showings, weekends included. Call either of us and one of us calls you back the same day. Most of the people we work with are moving within the area or relocating for work. We work in English and Portuguese."

Each one gives the reader a detail to repeat, names the situations the agent handles, explains how to reach them and puts credentials last. ## Bio mistakes: clichés and third person

Some phrases tell a reader nothing: "top producer," "dedicated to excellence," "your dream home," "passionate about real estate," "full-service," "results-driven." If you could swap in another agent's name and the sentence would still be true, cut it.

Plenty of agents write in third person ("Jess is a dedicated professional who...") because it feels less like bragging. On your own site it reads like a press release, and the referred client wants to hear from you. Use first person there. Keep a third-person version of 50 to 80 words for the places that expect one, like a brokerage roster, a seminar flyer or a podcast intro. If the brokerage roster is the only place your bio lives right now, [do realtors need their own website?](/blog/do-realtors-need-their-own-website/) covers what that page can and can't do for you.

A few more to cut: an opening about the market ("In today's competitive market..."), anything about who a neighborhood suits, and a row of designations with no explanation. For length, 150 to 300 words covers the main bio. If you have more to say, a longer About page with headings can hold it.

## How to write a real estate agent bio in one sitting

Write the four parts as four short paragraphs, in order, without editing as you go. Then read it out loud. Anything you wouldn't say to a client across a kitchen table comes out. Last, send it to one past client you trust and ask whether it sounds like you. If they say it's close, publish it, and put the date on your calendar to look at it again in six months.
